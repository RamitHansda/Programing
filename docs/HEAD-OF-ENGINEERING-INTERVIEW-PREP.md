## Interview Loop — What to Expect

Most companies running a Head of Engineering search will use a 3–5 round structure:

```
Round 1: HM / CTO Screening (60 min)
         → Career narrative, leadership philosophy, culture fit
         → "Walk me through what you've built and why it matters"

Round 2: System Design + Technical Depth (60–90 min)
         → Usually a production-scale problem in their domain
         → Tests: Can you still architect? Do you know the trade-offs cold?

Round 3: People & Org Leadership (60 min)
         → Behaviorals: hiring, performance, conflict, culture
         → Scenario-based: "You just joined, the team is underperforming — what do you do?"

Round 4: Cross-functional Round (60 min)
         → With a senior PM, VP of Product, or CEO
         → Tests: Do you think about the business, or just the tech?

Round 5: Executive / Culture Round (30–45 min)
         → CEO, co-founder, or board member
         → Tests: Ambition, values, long-term thinking, communication clarity
```

#### Design 1: Cross-Border Payments Platform (Your Owned System — Lead With This)

When asked "walk me through a system you own" or "design an international payments platform":

**Your 8-minute walkthrough:**

```
API Gateway → ALB → Funding Service
                        ↓ (Kafka: funding-topic)
              Invoice Service → Invoice Mapper
                        ↓ (Kafka: transaction-topic)
              Compliance Service (AML + Sanctions + Manual Review Dashboard)
                        ↓ (Kafka: approved-topic)
              SWIFT Service (batches by currency + VA provider)
                        ↓
              FX Service (Authorized Dealer Bank → Kafka: pricing-event)
                        ↓
              Pricing Engine (fee calculation → Kafka: settlement-event)
                        ↓
              Settlement Service → HDFC API
                        ↓
              Ledger Service + Notification Service
```

**The 3 hardest problems you solved:**

1. **Idempotency at every layer** — Redis distributed locks at Funding to deduplicate under retries. Every downstream service (Settlement, Ledger, Refund) has its own idempotency guarantee. Even the HDFC API call uses an idempotency key. Any operation can be retried safely.

2. **Vendor normalization** — Multiple vendors with different schemas. Funding Service has vendor-specific adapters that all produce the same canonical event. Downstream services are vendor-agnostic. Adding a new vendor = writing one new adapter.

3. **Async compliance without blocking throughput** — Compliance is mix of automated rules + manual review. Transactions wait in the async pipeline, they don't block new ones. Rejected transactions trigger the Refund Service with its own idempotency guarantee.

**The architectural trade-off you made:**

> "We chose a **modular monolith over microservices**. Domains are separated by module boundaries and communicate through Kafka topics — not direct calls. This gave us decoupling without the operational overhead of 15 independent services for a team of 12. It was the right call for our scale. I'd reconsider at 50K+ TPS where independent scaling matters more."

#### Design 2: Distributed Job Scheduler (Generic + Skydo)

High probability in interviews. You built one.

**Core components:**

```
Job Submission API
      ↓
Job Store (PostgreSQL — job definitions, state, schedule)
      ↓
Scheduler (polling + delay queue / time-wheel)
      ↓
Kafka Topic (job-execution-topic)
      ↓
Worker Pool (scalable, stateless)
      ↓
Execution Result → Job Store update + Dead Letter Queue for failures
```

**Key design decisions:**

| Decision | Your Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduler locking | Distributed lock (Redis SETNX with TTL) | Prevents double-dispatch across scheduler replicas |
| At-least-once vs exactly-once | At-least-once + idempotent workers | Exactly-once requires 2PC; at-least-once with idempotent job logic is simpler and more resilient |
| Failure handling | Retry with exponential backoff + DLQ after N attempts | Bounded retries prevent infinite loops; DLQ preserves jobs for investigation |
| Scale | Partition job-execution-topic by job type | Enables independent scaling of worker pools per job type |

### Technical Questions You Will Be Asked

**Q: How do you ensure distributed system reliability at scale?**

> "Three things I've implemented across every system I've owned: (1) Every mutation is idempotent by design — before anything else. (2) All async flows have a dead-letter queue with alerting — no silent failures. (3) State transitions emit structured events, not just logs — this is what makes distributed debugging tractable. At Skydo, we reduced production incidents by ~30% not by being more careful, but by making the system self-documenting and the failure modes observable."

**Q: PostgreSQL vs Kafka vs Redis — when do you use what?**

> "PostgreSQL is my default for transactional data where ACID guarantees are non-negotiable — payments, ledger entries, idempotency records. Kafka is for event streaming where you need durability, ordered delivery, and fan-out to multiple consumers — state transition events, audit logs, integration between services. Redis is for ephemeral data where latency is the priority — distributed locks, rate limiting, session state, hot caches. I've used all three together at Skydo: PostgreSQL for the payment record, Kafka for the state machine events, Redis for the idempotency lock."

**Q: How do you handle database migrations in a live payments system?**

> "Expand-contract pattern, always. Step 1 (Expand): add the new column or table, deploy code that writes to both old and new, reads from old. Step 2 (Backfill): migrate existing data — in batches with a rate limiter to avoid lock contention. Step 3 (Cut-over): flip reads to new column. Step 4 (Contract): drop the old column in a subsequent release. We never take downtime for schema changes — the biggest risk isn't the migration itself, it's the lock wait on a table with millions of rows."

## ROUND 3: People, Org & Culture

### Hiring

**Q: How do you hire senior engineers as Head of Engineering?**

> "Senior engineers hire themselves based on two things: the problem quality and the team quality. My job in hiring is to demonstrate both.
>
> In every senior interview I do, I spend as much time selling the problem as assessing the candidate. If I can't make a compelling case for why *this* engineering problem is worth their time — more so than the other 4 offers they have — I haven't done my job.
>
> On assessment: I care about three things in senior candidates. (1) Can they design systems under ambiguity? I give an open-ended design problem and watch how they handle incomplete requirements. (2) Do they have strong technical opinions they can defend and change? I deliberately push back on their design choices to see if they update on evidence or dig in out of ego. (3) Do they make the engineers around them better? I always talk to references who reported to them."

**Q: How do you handle a high-performer who is also difficult to work with?**

> "I separate 'difficult' into two categories. If it's high standards — they're pushing back on sloppy PRs, challenging bad architectural decisions, holding others accountable — that's not 'difficult,' that's exactly what a senior engineer should do. My job is to make sure they're doing it in a way that doesn't damage trust.
>
> If it's genuinely destructive behaviour — dismissing teammates publicly, not sharing context, taking credit — then even if they're technically brilliant, they're net negative to the team, and I act. I've had one case at Skydo. I had the direct conversation: here is the specific behaviour, here is the specific impact, here is what needs to change. I gave a 60-day window. The behaviour didn't change, and we parted ways. The team's reaction was relief, which told me I'd waited too long."

**Q: How do you build a bar-raising hiring process that scales?**

> "Three components: (1) A written scorecard per role — defined before we start interviewing, not shaped by who we interviewed. Every interviewer scores independently against the same rubric before calibration. (2) A calibration loop — we do a monthly review of interview-to-offer conversion rates and offer-to-performance correlations. If we're hiring people who struggle in their first 90 days, something in the process is miscalibrated. (3) Culture interviews done by senior ICs, not just managers — they're often better signals of team fit than executive interviews."

---

### Performance & Growth

**Q: How do you handle an underperforming engineer?**

> "Step 0: I check myself first. Is this a will problem or a skill problem? Is it a context problem — do they not understand what good looks like? Is it a system problem — are we making it too hard to succeed?
>
> Assuming I've answered those: I have a direct conversation with specific examples. Not 'your performance has been below expectations' but 'this PR review took 3 days and blocked two other engineers, this is the pattern I've seen 3 times in the last sprint.' Specifics are not unkind — vagueness is unkind, because it denies the person the information they need to improve.
>
> I then write down a clear improvement plan together — not imposed on them. I want them to author their own accountability. 60–90 days, explicit milestones, check-ins every two weeks. Most people actually want to succeed; what they lacked was clarity.
>
> If there's no improvement: I move to a PIP with HR, and if it still doesn't resolve, I make the separation decision. The most common mistake I've seen engineering leaders make is extending an underperformer's tenure past the point where the team can see it's being tolerated. It damages the culture more than the separation ever would."

---

### Org Design

**Q: How do you structure engineering teams as a company scales from 20 to 80 engineers?**

> "My default is Conway's Law in reverse — design the team structure to match the system architecture you want, not the one you have.
>
> At 20 engineers, a functional split (backend / frontend / infra / data) works fine. Coordination overhead is low; everyone knows everyone.
>
> At 40–60 engineers, you need product-aligned squads — each squad owns a domain end-to-end (product, backend, infra for that domain). Full-stack ownership means fewer inter-team dependencies and faster delivery. The cost is some redundancy in skills — you have infra expertise replicated across squads. Worth it.
>
> Platform team appears at 50+ engineers. The platform team owns developer experience, CI/CD, observability, shared infra. Without it, each squad reinvents the wheel and you accumulate infrastructure inconsistency. At Skydo we were effectively 12 engineers but I played the platform function myself — at 50 engineers you need a dedicated team.
>
> The mistake I see most often: companies go to squads too late, then try to reorg everything at once under delivery pressure. The reorg itself kills velocity for a quarter. I'd rather introduce squad structure incrementally, domain by domain, before it becomes an emergency."

**Q: How do you handle the tension between speed and technical debt?**

> "I don't frame it as tension — I frame it as a portfolio allocation decision.
>
> My default allocation: 70% new feature work, 20% reliability and platform work, 10% debt reduction. Non-negotiable. The moment you let debt reduction go to zero, you're borrowing from your future delivery capacity at compound interest.
>
> I also distinguish types of debt. There's **deliberate debt** — we knew we were cutting a corner and documented why. That's fine and occasionally necessary. There's **accidental debt** — we didn't know it was a corner until later. That's a process signal, not a moral failure. And there's **liability debt** — this will cause a production incident or a security breach. That goes to the front of the queue and I will defend that prioritisation to any PM in the company."

## ROUND 4: Cross-Functional + Business Thinking

### Stakeholder Management

**Q: How do you manage a CEO who keeps adding scope mid-sprint?**

> "I've been in this exact situation at Skydo.
>
> The first thing I do is make the cost visible and specific — not abstract. Not 'this will slow us down.' But 'this adds 4 engineer-days. We're currently 3 days from shipping the compliance feature that unblocks our banking partner. If we add this, we miss that window by a week, which means a delay in our next cohort onboarding. You decide — I'll execute either way.'
>
> I've found that CEOs who add scope mid-sprint are usually doing it because they don't have a clear view of what's in flight and what the trade-offs are. The solution isn't to fight the additions — it's to give them better visibility so they can make informed calls. Weekly engineering status that's written for a non-technical reader, a public capacity dashboard, a roadmap that shows what's in and what's out — these structural solutions work better than individual negotiations."

**Q: How do you earn the trust of a non-technical CEO?**

> "Three things I've found that build trust with non-technical executives faster than anything else:
>
> (1) **Translate proactively.** Don't wait for them to ask 'how does this affect the business.' Tell them. When I made the call to switch from microservices to a modular monolith at Skydo, I wrote a one-page brief for the CEO: 'we are making this change, here is why it matters for delivery velocity, here is the risk we're managing, here is how we'll know if it's working.' He never had to ask.
>
> (2) **Own bad news early.** The worst thing an engineering leader can do is let a CEO find out about a production incident or a delivery miss from someone else. I have a personal policy: if something affects customers or a key delivery date, the CEO hears it from me first, within the hour.
>
> (3) **Be the person who fixes problems, not manages them.** Non-technical executives lose trust in engineering leaders who always explain why something is hard and rarely bring solutions. I try to show up with a recommendation, not just a problem."

---

### Product Thinking

**Q: How do you approach building technical roadmaps in alignment with business goals?**

> "I start by making sure I understand the business model and where it's under the most pressure. At Skydo, the business pressure was: our compliance review SLA was blocking activation velocity, which directly affected revenue. Engineering's response was not to ask 'what features should we build' but 'how do we remove this bottleneck.'
>
> My process: (1) Start with business OKRs for the next 6–12 months. (2) Identify the 3 engineering constraints that are most limiting to those OKRs. (3) Build a technical roadmap around removing those constraints — not around feature requests. Features are the product team's job. Removing engineering-layer constraints is mine.
>
> I present this as a prioritized matrix: business impact (high/medium/low) × engineering effort (high/medium/low) × dependency risk (blocks what?). Stakeholders can see the trade-offs and make informed calls."

## ROUND 5: Executive / Founder Round

### The CEO Calibration Questions

**"What makes a great Head of Engineering, in your view?"**

> "Three things that I don't see commonly combined:
>
> The ability to zoom in and zoom out. Great engineering leaders can walk a board through the 2-year platform strategy and then two hours later pair with an engineer on an architecture decision. The leaders who can only do one of these are either disconnected from their team's reality or stuck in the weeds.
>
> The ability to hire people who are better than them and not feel threatened. The measure of a Head of Engineering is not their personal technical output — it's the aggregate output of the team they build. If your best engineers are waiting for your approval on technical decisions, you've failed.
>
> The ability to hold the line on technical quality under business pressure, without being a blocker. This is the hardest one. The CEO wants to ship in 2 weeks. The right answer is 4 weeks if you want a system that won't burn down. A great engineering leader negotiates that, explains the risk clearly, and doesn't just fold — but also doesn't just say no."

**"Where do you want to be in 5 years?"**

> "Honestly: leading the engineering function for a company that has scaled from growth stage to market leader, with a platform and a team I'm proud of. Whether that's CTO or Head of Engineering or VP, I don't care about the title. I care about the scope and the problem quality.
>
> I also want to have built engineers who go on to lead engineering teams of their own. The people I've mentored directly — I measure my career success partly by how far they go. That's not altruism, it's how you build a reputation that attracts the next generation of strong engineers to work for you."

**"What's your biggest weakness as a leader?"**

> "I move fast and I assume others have the same context I do. Earlier in my career I'd make an architectural call and announce it — I'd done the analysis, I was confident, I thought it was obvious. What I missed was that the team hadn't been in the same conversations I had, so the decision felt arbitrary to them, not reasoned.
>
> I've gotten much better at working in public — writing up the thinking, sharing draft decisions before they're final, inviting challenge. The slower decision-making is worth it: you get better decisions and better buy-in, which is ultimately faster delivery."
